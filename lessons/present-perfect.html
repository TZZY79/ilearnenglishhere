Why this matters:Present perfect talks about your whole life up to right now — never a single finished moment like "yesterday." That's the whole idea of this lesson.
📚 Acquire

What is the Present Perfect?

It's for an experience that happened at some point in your life — and it's still relevant now. We don't say exactly when.

PASTsomething happened (we don't know when)NOW / TODAY
Subject+have / has+Past Participle
She has eaten sushi.he / she / it → HAS
We have visited Paris.I / you / we / they → HAVE
❓
"Why not just use past simple?"Past simple ("I ate a hamburger") needs a finished, specific time — real or implied. Present perfect ("I have eaten a hamburger") has no specific time — it's about your life experience up to now. Remember our vote: "I have never eaten a hamburger" ← that's this exact idea!
⚡ Quick Rule

Have vs Has

Tap a pronoun to see which verb it needs.

← Tap a pronoun above to see the rule in action.
🧠
How to remember it:Only he / she / it (one person or thing) use HAS. Everyone else — I, you, we, they, or any plural — uses HAVE. Same rule as present simple's -s ending, just backwards in feel!
🔧 Past Participle

Regular & Irregular Verbs

Tap each card to reveal its past participle. Green tag = regular (+ed, easy). Red tag = irregular (must memorise).

❓
"Isn't past participle the same as past simple?"For regular verbs, yes — both are "played," "visited." For irregular verbs, often no: go → went (past simple) but go → gone (past participle). Present perfect always uses the past participle column, never past simple.

🌍 Life Experiences

Ever vs Never

Tap a question to see its matching answer.

❗
The one rule that trips people up:Ever = "at any time in your life" — used only in questions. Never = "at no time in your life" — it looks positive (no "not"/"don't") but it already means negative, so don't add another "not"!
🕐 Just · For · Since

Just, For & Since

Tap each example to see what kind of time word it uses, and why.

⏳
"What's the actual difference between FOR and SINCE?"For + a length of time (3 years, 6 months, ages). Since + a starting point (2020, Monday, I arrived). Quick test: if you can count it, use FOR. If it's a point on the calendar or clock, use SINCE.
