The Story of English

How English was born, and where it's going.

English is not just grammar — it's 1,500 years of invasions, kings, poets, sailors, dictionaries, and today, the internet. Knowing the story makes the strange parts of English (like spelling!) finally make sense. Read one chapter a day.

Then & now

👑 The King's English vs everyday English

You might hear "the Queen's English" — since 2022, with King Charles III, people say "the King's English". It usually means very "correct", careful British English, often spoken with an accent called Received Pronunciation (RP) — the accent of royals, old BBC newsreaders and some private schools. Here's the surprise: only a small minority of British people (often estimated at around 2–3%) actually speak with RP.

Most Brits speak with regional accents — and they're proud of them: Brummie (Birmingham), Scouse (Liverpool), Geordie (Newcastle), Cockney (East London), Mancunian (Manchester), Yorkshire, Glaswegian, Welsh and many more. Young Londoners often speak Multicultural London English, with words like "bare" (= lots) and "innit".

In the 1950s, writers even made lists of "posh" (upper-class) and "common" words. Some still carry a class feeling today:

Cockney rhyming slang is a famous "common people" invention: you say a phrase that rhymes with the word you mean. "Apples and pears" = stairs. "Dog and bone" = phone. "I'm going up the apples!"

💡 What should learners do? Understand both! Use clear standard English in exams and at work, but learn the everyday version too — it's what you'll actually hear in shops, pubs and on the street.

The experience

🇬🇧 The British Way — what people REALLY mean

This is the part textbooks don't teach. British people are famous for understatement (saying less than they mean), politeness (avoiding a direct "no"), self-deprecation (joking about themselves) and banter (friendly teasing). Learn these and you'll understand Brits — and they'll love talking to you.
